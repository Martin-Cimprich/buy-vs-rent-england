  {Martin Cimprich\footnote{School of Economics and Finance, Queen Mary University of London. Email: \href{mailto:m.cimprich@qmul.ac.uk}{m.cimprich@qmul.ac.uk}.    \normalsize Queen Mary University of London}
  {This version: September 2026}
  {I compare buying a first home with renting and investing, in a cash-flow-matched monthly simulation on official English data from January 2005 to June 2026. The buyer takes a 10\% deposit on a five-year fix, with maintenance and depreciation from the national accounts and first-time-buyer stamp duty; the renter occupies the same dwelling and invests the deposit-equivalent, plus every monthly difference in housing costs, in a global equity index fund, which the comparison assumes they keep up throughout. Price and rent share one population-weighted regional composition, since the official national averages weight regions differently and pairing them biases the result. Over 199 cohorts each holding five years the two strategies finish level, buying ahead in 51\% of cohorts at a median renter/owner wealth ratio of 0.98. Buying wins more often at longer holds, 62\% at seven years and 61\% at ten, but the median renter still finishes within 8--11\% of the owner, \gbp{14{,}454} short over a decade. The mean ratio is 1.22, while the mean gap of \gbp{1{,}744} favours the owner, because renting's proportional wins fell in cohorts where the owner's equity had collapsed, peaking at 4.17 in November 2007. Ownership is competitive only because of the mortgage, since a cash buyer wins 8\% of cohorts, but the same leverage left sixteen northern cohorts entering in 2007--08 with negative equity. At June 2026 a five-year buyer needs 2.3\% appreciation to match equities earning a cautious 5.9\%, and 3.2\% to match the 10.4\% they actually delivered; the dwelling itself appreciated at 3.2\%.}
