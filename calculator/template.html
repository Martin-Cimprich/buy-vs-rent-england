What it works out

Two ways of paying for the same home, compared pound for pound. The buyer puts down a deposit, pays purchase costs and stamp duty, then a monthly mortgage payment plus maintenance. The renter starts by investing exactly what the buyer put down, pays the market rent, and each month invests the difference between the owner's costs and the rent (or withdraws it, when the rent is higher). At the end the buyer's wealth is the home's value less selling costs and the outstanding mortgage; the renter's is the portfolio.

Where the numbers come from

House prices from the UK House Price Index (HM Land Registry); rents from the ONS Price Index of Private Rents; mortgage rates from Bank of England quoted five-year fixed rates at 75%, 90% and 95% loan-to-value; investment returns from the MSCI ACWI global index in sterling, net of tax and a 0.12% fund fee; and the exact England first-time-buyer stamp-duty schedule in force in each month since 2005. Data runs to June 2026.

"England (representative)" is a population-weighted average of the nine English regions' prices and rents, so both sides share one regional composition. Using the published national averages instead would understate the price-to-rent ratio, because prices are weighted by transactions and rents by tenancies.

What it leaves out

Taxes, on the grounds that a first-time buyer's home is free of capital-gains tax and the renter's contributions fit inside ISA allowances for most households. Council tax and utilities, which the occupant pays either way. Moving costs. And everything non-financial: security of tenure, freedom to renovate, the flexibility to leave, and the discipline needed to actually invest the difference every single month. The renter's side of these results assumes that discipline throughout.

This is not financial advice. It shows how the arithmetic works and how it would have played out on past data. Your circumstances, and the future, will differ.
